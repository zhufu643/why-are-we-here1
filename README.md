# Why Designers Should Understand Programming
Today, AI systems are capable of generating executable code from only a few natural-language instructions, enabling creators to rapidly develop simple websites, applications, and games. As a result, learning programming may appear increasingly unnecessary or even outdated. However, as a designer, I believe that learning programming remains essential. Programming should not be viewed merely as a constantly evolving technology that may eventually be replaced by newer tools. Rather, it should be understood as a language and a medium—an essential bridge between human intention and artificial intelligence. A solid understanding of programming languages allows us to communicate our ideas to AI with greater clarity and precision, ultimately shaping how effectively and independently we can make use of these powerful tools in the digital age.

Throughout history, the emergence of new technologies has repeatedly transformed the ways in which people create and communicate. The development of the typewriter, printing technologies, and computers gradually reduced the need for traditional scribes, yet neither writers nor written language disappeared. Text has continued to serve as a fundamental medium through which writers express ideas, while technological innovation has primarily changed the tools and processes involved in writing. Computers can help writers record, revise, and distribute their work more efficiently, but they cannot replace the writer’s role in deciding what should ultimately be expressed. I believe the relationship between designers and AI is similar. AI may assist in generating code, but designers must still determine what to create, why it should be created, and what kind of experience or impact the final work should produce. In other word, learning programming is comparable to learning a language. Although AI has changed the way we work and can now help us to “speak” or structure this language, we still need the ability to read and understand it. Without that understanding, it becomes difficult to evaluate whether AI-generated results accurately reflect our original design intentions. Ultimately, the responsibility for defining the meaning, purpose, and direction of a project still belongs to the designer.

In The Art of Code, Dylan Beattie argues that code is not merely a technical tool for solving practical problems or improving efficiency. Through examples such as generative art, programming languages, and live coding, he presents a broader understanding of programming: people may write code not only to accomplish a specific function, but also to explore ideas, express themselves, and create, much like artists working with paint, sound, or visual imagery.
From this perspective, programming can be understood as a creative medium through which artists and designers communicate and realize their ideas. Just as painters must understand the properties of color, composition, and materials, designers increasingly need to understand code as a kind of digital “material.” As digital systems become central environments for design and artistic practice, familiarity with programming allows creators to better understand the possibilities, limitations, and expressive potential of the medium itself.Future designers may no longer need to write every line of code by hand, just as artists are not required to produce their own canvases, paints, or brushes. However, this does not mean that designers can entirely avoid understanding programming languages. In an era when AI can generate code rapidly, a digital media designer who is unable to read or interpret AI-generated code may struggle to determine whether the output accurately reflects their design intentions or to identify potential logical errors, technical limitations, and unintended consequences. Canadian media theorist Marshall McLuhan famously described media as “extensions of man.” Programming can similarly be understood as an extension of human thought, as it translates abstract and often ambiguous ideas into structured rules that computers can interpret and execute. From this perspective, learning programming is not simply about mastering correct syntax; more importantly, it involves developing the ability to organize and structure ideas. Through programming, designers repeatedly practice breaking down complex concepts into clear instructions, logical relationships, and interconnected systems. In this sense, programming cultivates more than technical competence. It also strengthens logical reasoning, systems thinking, problem-solving, and creative expression which are all increasingly important abilities for designers working in digital environments.

AI may transform the way we write code, but it does not diminish the value of learning programming. For designers, coding remains a way to understand how digital systems work and to translate ideas into clear, executable structures. Although AI can generate code on our behalf, designers must still decide what they want to create, why it matters, and whether the final result truly reflects their intentions.
